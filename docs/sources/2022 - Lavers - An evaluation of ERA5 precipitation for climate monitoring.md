# An evaluation of ERA5 precipitation for climate monitoring

- **Authors, year, venue:** Lavers, D. A., Simmons, A., Vamborg, F., Rodwell, M. J., 2022. Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society 148(748), 3152–3165.
- **Link:** [10.1002/qj.4351](https://doi.org/10.1002/qj.4351)
- **Access:** open access, CC BY-NC-ND (Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivs).
- **Read:** full text. The parts that matter for the bridge are sections 2.1–2.3 and Table 1 (gauges, how the daily ERA5 total is built from the hourly forecasts, scores), 3.1 and Figs 2–3 (bias and random error by month), 3.2 and Fig. 4 (SEEPS), 3.3 and Figs 5–6 (four extreme events, annual maxima), 3.4 and Figs 7–8 (monthly correlation), 3.5 (representativeness) and 4 (conclusions). Figures were checked on the rendered pages for the values printed in their titles and legends.

## What it is

A global check of ERA5 daily precipitation against 24 h totals from 5,637 SYNOP gauges sent over the WMO GTS, for 2001–2020, written to support the monthly climate bulletins of the Copernicus Climate Change Service. ERA5 (31 km, hourly short-range forecasts) is matched to each gauge by nearest neighbour on a 0.25° grid. The scores are the mean and standard deviation of ERA5 minus gauge for each month, the SEEPS score (dry / light / heavy), annual maximum 1-day totals (RX1), four case studies and seasonal correlations of monthly anomalies. The paper does not look at trends, time homogeneity or sub-daily precipitation.

## Numbers we use

- **Gauge sample:** section 2.1 and Fig. 1 (pp. 3154–3155). 5,637 stations; 24 h totals ending at 00, 01, 03, 04/05, 06 or 12 UTC (3,882 stations at 00 UTC). Values below 0 mm or above 500 mm are removed. A station needs 50 % of daily data over the whole period and within each season. The totals are raw: no correction for wind undercatch or unheated gauges in snow.
- **How the daily ERA5 total is built:** section 2.2 and Table 1 (p. 3154). ERA5 precipitation is a forecast, not an analysis. The hourly fields come from the forecasts started at 06 and 18 UTC, at lead times of 1 to 12 h. A 00–24 UTC day uses steps T+7 to T+12 of the previous 18 UTC run, T+1 to T+12 of the 06 UTC run and T+1 to T+6 of the 18 UTC run. ERA5 assimilates no rain gauges anywhere; it assimilates radar–gauge composites over the United States east of the Rockies from 2009 onwards. No such input is mentioned for Europe.
- **Wet bias, all stations:** section 3.1 and Fig. 2 (p. 3156). The mean ERA5-minus-gauge difference is +0.20 mm day⁻¹ in January, +0.26 in April, +0.14 in July and +0.19 in October (panel titles). The paper attributes the wet bias mainly to ERA5 putting precipitation on days that are dry at the gauge. It gives no number for the wet-day frequency itself. The biases are spatially coherent between neighbouring stations. On the figure, most stations in western and central Europe are in the wet classes (+0.05 to +2.5 mm day⁻¹) in January; in July the colours are mixed (reading of the colour scale, not a number in the text).
- **Random error by season:** section 3.1 and Fig. 3 (p. 3157). The mean standard deviation of the daily differences is 4.83 mm day⁻¹ in January, 4.80 in April, 6.11 in July and 5.19 in October (panel titles). The smallest errors, below 2.5 mm day⁻¹, are in the winter extratropics, northern Europe included. The largest, above 15 mm day⁻¹, are in the tropics and follow the ITCZ. The reason given: winter precipitation comes from large-scale extratropical cyclones, which ERA5 resolves well, while summer convection is poorly resolved and "less predictable even at ranges <12 hr".
- **SEEPS (discrimination between dry, light and heavy days):** section 2.3 (p. 3155), section 3.2 and Fig. 4 (pp. 3157–3158). A day is dry at ≤ 0.2 mm; light days are defined to occur twice as often as heavy days in each station's monthly 2001–2020 climatology. Stations with more than 99 % dry days are excluded (134 in January, 90 in April, 301 in July, 101 in October). Skill is highest in the winter extratropics, Europe in January among the best, and drops from winter to summer in the Northern Hemisphere extratropics, though it stays above tropical values. High SEEPS goes with low standard deviation.
- **Extremes, annual maxima (RX1):** section 3.3 and Fig. 6 (pp. 3160–3161). Over 105,191 station-years, 72.1 % of the points lie below the 1:1 line, i.e. ERA5 has the smaller annual maximum. Mean of the fitted GEV distributions: 66.0 mm day⁻¹ for gauges and 43.6 for ERA5 over all stations; 108.1 and 63.3 in the tropics (20° S–20° N); 52.3 and 36.5 in the extratropics (35–65° N and S, Fig. 6b,c legends). In the extratropics ERA5 therefore reaches about 70 % of the gauge maxima (ratio computed here). Part of the gap is ascribed to representativeness. Gauge values cluster at 200 and 300 mm day⁻¹, a sign of reporting artefacts in the observed extremes.
- **Four extreme events:** section 3.3 and Fig. 5 (pp. 3158–3160). Patterns agree in general, magnitudes do not. Tropical cyclone Oswald (2013): mean error −35.36 mm, SD 47.98 mm (N = 36). Storm Desmond (2015): mean −0.47 mm, SD 26.55 mm (N = 86); one Lake District gauge had 201.0 mm against 49.9 mm in ERA5, orographic enhancement missed. Storm Alex (2020): mean +6.03 mm, SD 19.77 mm (N = 171); too much precipitation north of the Alps, where the ERA5 grid is on average 199.9 m higher than the 51 stations in 46.5–47.5° N, 6–11° E. Flood of July 2021 in Germany and Belgium (24 h ending 00 UTC on 15 July 2021): mean +2.30 mm, SD 8.46 mm (N = 214), the best match of the four.
- **Monthly anomalies:** section 2.3 (p. 3155), section 3.4, Figs 7–8 (pp. 3161–3162). Spearman correlation of monthly anomalies, 31 to 60 values per season. At Saarbrücken (Germany, 97.2 % daily availability) it ranges from 0.80 in JJA to 0.98 in DJF; at Singapore from 0.50 (SON) to 0.60 (DJF). Over all stations, Europe in DJF is among the highest; extratropical correlation drops in summer.
- **Representativeness:** section 3.5 (p. 3162). A 0.25° cell covers about 1,000 km², against a point gauge. At the 5,610 stations with an elevation, the mean station height is 368.1 m and the mean ERA5 grid height 424.7 m, 56.6 m higher. The authors suggest that part of the wet bias may come from this offset and from orographic enhancement.
- **Sub-daily data:** section 4 (p. 3163). Hourly gauge totals reach ECMWF from about 3,000 stations via the GTS; they were not used. An hourly evaluation of intense precipitation is left for future work.

## Relevance to DECADE

H3 (#20), choice and use of ERA5-Land as the bridge between ODYSSEY and CIRRUS. ERA5-Land precipitation is the ERA5 field interpolated to 9 km (Muñoz-Sabater et al., 2021), so these findings apply to it directly, at ERA5's 31 km effective resolution.

- **Matching the wet frequency first is needed.** The wet bias of ERA5 comes mostly from precipitation on days that are dry at the gauge (section 3.1). A fixed small threshold would therefore give the bridge too many wet hours compared with the radar, and the excess would leak into the level comparison. Matching the wet frequency before comparing amounts removes this effect. The paper gives no frequency number to set the threshold; that has to come from other studies (for example Wood et al., 2025) or from the data.
- **Seasonal calibration fits the error structure.** Bias, random error, SEEPS and correlation all change with the season in the extratropics (Figs 2–4, 8). Winter is where ERA5 is closest to the gauges in Europe; summer, with convection, is where it is weakest (July SD 6.11 against 4.83 mm day⁻¹ in January, all stations). Seasonal factors are thus the right unit, and the JJA factor of the bridge will be the least certain.
- **Upper quantiles are biased low.** In the extratropics the ERA5 annual maxima are about 70 % of the gauge ones (52.3 vs 36.5 mm day⁻¹, Fig. 6). Matching radar quantiles in the tail directly to ERA5-Land would pull the radar extremes down. The bridge can still work for the tail if it is used in a relative way: the same ERA5 tail deficit sits on both sides of July 2024 and cancels when ODYSSEY and CIRRUS are each referred to it. This holds only if the deficit is stable in time, which the paper does not test.
- **No evidence on stability across 2024.** The paper pools 2001–2020 and reports no trend, break or change in the observing system for Europe; the one input change it names (US radar–gauge composites from 2009) is outside our domain. The homogeneity of ERA5 precipitation in 2024 must be checked from other sources or tested in our data.
- **Belgium-relevant evidence is favourable.** Northern and western Europe are among the best regions for bias, random error and SEEPS, especially in winter (Figs 2–4). The nearest single station shown, Saarbrücken, has monthly correlations of 0.80–0.98 (Fig. 7). The July 2021 flood, which hit Belgium, is the best of the four events (SD 8.46 mm, Fig. 5d). Belgium is low-lying, so the orographic errors seen at the Alps and the Lake District are less of a concern, except perhaps in the Ardennes.
- **Hourly values are short forecasts.** Each hour of ERA5 (and so ERA5-Land) precipitation is a 1–12 h forecast from the 06 or 18 UTC run (Table 1). The paper says convective precipitation is hard to predict even at these ranges, but it does not evaluate hourly values or look for steps where the source run changes. A 5 or 15 min radar compared with hourly ERA5-Land should therefore be aggregated to at least hourly, and daily comparisons are the level at which this paper supports ERA5.

## Limits

- **Daily gauges at 24 h:** all scores are for 24 h totals ending at various UTC hours (mostly 00 UTC) or for monthly means. Nothing is said about the hourly distribution, the wet-hour frequency or the diurnal cycle that a 5 or 15 min radar calibration needs.
- **Raw point gauges, not radar:** the reference has undercatch and snow errors (section 2.1), and the point-to-grid mismatch (section 3.5) inflates the random error and the tail deficit. Neither transfers one-to-one to a radar-to-grid comparison, where both fields are areal.
- **Global scale:** results are shown as world maps for four mid-season months (January, April, July, October) and as all-station means. No number is given for Belgium or the Benelux; regional statements here come from reading the colour scales.
- **Period 2001–2020:** the study ends before the ODYSSEY to CIRRUS change of July 2024, pools all years, and gives no time series of the errors except monthly anomalies at two stations for 2016–2020 (Fig. 7b,d). It says nothing on homogeneity or trends.
- **ERA5, not ERA5-Land:** the paper uses ERA5 at 0.25° with nearest-neighbour matching. ERA5-Land interpolates the same field to 0.1°, which adds no information but changes the matching scale.
- **No wet-frequency number:** the wet bias is explained through dry-day overestimation, but no wet-day frequency of ERA5 against the gauges is reported, and the only threshold used (0.2 mm day⁻¹ for a dry day) comes from SEEPS.
