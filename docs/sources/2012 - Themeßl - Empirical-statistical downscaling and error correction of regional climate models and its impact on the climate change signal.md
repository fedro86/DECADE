# Empirical-statistical downscaling and error correction of regional climate models and its impact on the climate change signal

- **Authors, year, venue:** Themeßl, M. J., Gobiet, A., Heinrich, G., 2012. Climatic Change 112, 449–468 (published online 23 September 2011).
- **Link:** [10.1007/s10584-011-0224-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-011-0224-4)
- **Access:** free PDF on SpringerLink (downloaded without login on 11/11/2026); © Springer Science+Business Media B.V. 2011, no open licence stated.
- **Read:** full text; sections 2.2.1 (quantile mapping, Eqs. 1–3), 2.2.2 (frequency adaptation, Fig. 2), 2.2.3 (new extremes, Fig. 3), 3.1–3.2 (cross-validated evaluation, Tables 1 and 3), 3.3 (impact on the climate change signal, Table 4), 4 (conclusions and caveats). The electronic supplementary material was not read.

## What it is

The paper evaluates empirical quantile mapping (QM) of daily precipitation and temperature from the CCLM regional model (25 km, ENSEMBLES) against E-OBS (0.25°) over Europe, 1961–2000, and applies it to an A1B scenario up to 2050. It adds two extensions to the basic method: *frequency adaptation* (FA), for a model with more dry days than the observations, and a constant extrapolation of the correction for values outside the calibration range. It then measures how much QM changes the climate change signal.

## Numbers we use

- **Additive correction per day of year and grid cell:** section 2.2.1, Eqs. (1)–(3). `P = ecdf_mod,cal(X_raw)` (Eq. 3); `CF = ecdf_obs,cal⁻¹(P) − ecdf_mod,cal⁻¹(P)` (Eq. 2); `Y_cor = X_raw + CF` (Eq. 1). The ECDFs are empirical (no fitted distribution) and are built separately for each day of the year and each grid cell. The method is distribution-based, so it needs no temporal pairing between model and observations (section 3.1).
- **31-day moving window:** section 2.2.1. The ECDF for a given day of the year is built from a 31-day window centred on that day, pooled over all calibration years.
- **Frequency adaptation, when it is needed:** section 2.2.2. If the model has more dry days than the observations (`ecdf_mod,cal(0) > ecdf_obs,cal(0)`), plain QM maps every model dry day to `ecdf_obs,cal⁻¹(ecdf_mod,cal(0))`, the first wet class of the observations, and creates a systematic wet bias. In the paper this happens with the summer drying of RCMs in south-eastern Europe; the more common case is the opposite ("drizzling effect").
- **Frequency adaptation, procedure:** section 2.2.2. (1) Compute the fraction `P0 = (ecdf_mod,cal(0) − ecdf_obs,cal(0)) / ecdf_mod,cal(0)`, i.e. the share of model dry days that should be wet. (2) Pick that fraction of the model dry days at random. (3) Give each picked day an amount obtained by linear interpolation between 0 and `ecdf_obs,cal⁻¹(ecdf_mod,cal(0))`; the other model dry days stay at zero. Model wet days follow Eqs. (1)–(3). After FA the dry-day fraction equals the observed one, since `ecdf_mod(0)·(1 − P0) = ecdf_obs(0)`. The text does not detail how the interpolation weight is drawn. Example: Fig. 2 (1 July, one grid cell in Eastern Europe, 1 mm/d bins); FA removes the summer wet bias of plain QM in Eastern Europe (Fig. 2c–d).
- **No wet-day threshold for QM:** sections 2.2.2 and 3.1. Dry means zero in the ECDF; no threshold is stated for the mapping. The thresholds in Table 2 (≥ 1 mm/d for `pint`, ≥ 10 mm/d for `pn10`) belong to the evaluation indices, not to QM; 0.1–1 mm/d is the "drizzling" class in section 3.1.
- **Extrapolation beyond the calibration range:** section 2.2.3. QMv1a keeps constant the correction (difference between observed and modelled ECDF) found at the highest and lowest calibration quantiles. QMv1b does the same but ignores the three highest/lowest correction terms, assuming noisy tails. QMv0 (no extrapolation) maps every new extreme to the calibration maximum and underestimates them strongly (Fig. 3).
- **Test of extrapolation:** section 2.2.3, Fig. 3. ERA-40-driven hindcast; at each grid cell the 5 years with the highest observed annual maxima are left out of calibration and their annual maxima are compared. QMv1a and QMv1b both produce new extremes and partly beat the uncorrected model; QMv1a is slightly better (smooth tails of the CCLM correction functions) and is used in the rest of the paper. A residual overestimation of the upper tail remains, because allowing new extremes also lets model outliers through.
- **Why not a fitted distribution:** section 2.2.3. It would lose information compared with the empirical ECDF and would need a priori assumptions on the shape of the pdf.
- **30 years to calibrate, 10 to validate:** section 3.1. Decadal leave-one-out cross-validation: each decade of 1961–2000 is corrected with QM calibrated on the other 30 years. With the 31-day window this means about 30 × 31 = 930 days per ECDF (our arithmetic). E-OBS cells need at least 80% data availability in 1961–2000 (section 2.1).
- **Bias reduction:** section 3.1, Table 1. Uncorrected precipitation biases range from −0.7 to +1.5 mm/d (about −40% to +70%); after QM with FA they are ≤ 0.1 mm/d per sub-region and ≤ 0.5 mm/d per grid cell. The conclusions (section 4) state that this result for precipitation is reached "only after frequency adaptation".
- **Where QM stops working well:** section 3.1 and section 4. Intensities below 30 mm/d are well adjusted; above about the 99th percentile the frequency is still often overestimated. For maximum 1-day precipitation (`px1d`) the residual sub-regional bias is up to 4.4 mm/d (19.5 mm/d per grid cell), with overestimation above about 75 mm/d (section 3.2, Table 3).
- **Change of the climate change signal:** section 3.3, Table 4, Figs. 9–10. Monthly signals 1971–2000 → 2021–2050. Mean precipitation: changes in the annual cycles below 0.1 mm/d; mean temperature: ≤ 0.3 K per sub-region. Derived indices change more: the signal of `pint` in Iberia in July drops by about 60%, autumn signals of `pint` and `px1d` in Iberia reach up to 200% of the uncorrected ones, threshold indices (summer days, tropical nights) change by up to 80%. Significance (Wilcoxon, 95%) mostly unchanged.
- **Cause of that change:** section 4. A correction that depends on magnitude, combined with a trend that shifts the future distribution into a different part of the correction function.
- **Other caveats:** section 4. Errors in temporal sequence are not corrected (but not degraded); each variable is corrected separately, so physical consistency and autocorrelation may be distorted; errors are expected to be larger when QM is applied to single decades, because of decadal variability.

## Relevance to DECADE

H3 (#20), harmonisation of ODYSSEY and CIRRUS through a bridge dataset.

- **Frequency matched first, then quantile mapping:** FA is the published procedure for the case where the product to be corrected is drier than its reference. This case is plausible in H3, because ERA5-Land and CERRA-Land are reanalyses that may have more wet hours or days than a radar product aggregated to their grid; the direction must be checked per season and per product. When the product is wetter than the reference, the usual rule applies: values in the dry part of the reference CDF are set to zero (Gudmundsson et al. 2012, section 2.2). FA needs only `ecdf_mod(0)`, `ecdf_obs(0)` and one quantile, all computable beforehand, so it fits the real-time constraint; its random step, however, makes the output non-deterministic, which would need a decision (for example a fixed seed or a deterministic rule) before use.
- **Seasonal windows:** the paper uses a 31-day moving window per day of year, with 30 calibration years. This is the literature reference for the window choice in H3. With the short CIRRUS record, a 31-day window pools only a few years, so each CIRRUS ECDF rests on far fewer independent events than in the paper; longer seasonal windows trade that against a coarser annual cycle. The paper does not compare window lengths.
- **Extremes:** the constant extrapolation of the last correction (QMv1a), and the variant that drops the three most extreme correction terms (QMv1b), are the two tested options for radar values above the calibration range. The paper keeps QMv1a, but notes that QMv1b is meant for noisy tails, which is more likely with a short CIRRUS record.
- **Additive correction:** Eq. (1) corrects by adding a difference of quantiles. For a comparison in reflectivity (dB) an additive correction corresponds to a multiplicative one in rain rate; for a comparison in mm/h the additive and multiplicative forms extrapolate differently. The paper uses the additive form only.
- **Pitfall:** the change of the climate change signal (section 3.3) is the analogue of a risk for DECADE: a magnitude-dependent correction calibrated on one period can modify trends between ODYSSEY years and CIRRUS years, mostly for threshold-based indices such as exceedance counts.

## Limits

- Daily precipitation from a 25 km model against 0.25° gridded gauges, not 5 min or 15 min radar reflectivity composites at 1–2 km compared on a 5.5–9 km bridge grid.
- Calibration uses 30 years of full overlap between model and reference. In H3 each product is calibrated against the bridge separately; CIRRUS starts only in 7/2024 and the direct ODYSSEY–CIRRUS overlap is 4 days, so the sample per window is much smaller than in the paper and the tails will be noisier.
- The window length (31 days) and the extrapolation choice (QMv1a) are taken without a sensitivity test on alternatives.
- FA is shown on one example cell and on bias maps for Eastern Europe in summer; there is no quantitative test of the random interpolation rule itself.
- The model–reference error here comes from model physics; in H3 the difference between products comes from compositing, resolution and calibration of the radar network, which may vary in time differently from a model bias.
