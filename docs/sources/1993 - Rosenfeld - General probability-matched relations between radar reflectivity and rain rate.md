## What it is

The paper presents the probability matching method (PMM): a relation between radar reflectivity Ze and rain rate R is built by pairing values of equal cumulative probability in the two distributions, not by fitting collocated pairs. The rain/no-rain split is matched first, on collocated and simultaneous radar and gauge data, and the conditional distributions are then matched rank by rank, with no time pairing. It is applied to the NOAA–TOGA C-band radar and 22 tipping-bucket gauges near Darwin (Australia) in the 1987–88 wet season, stratified by four rain regimes and four range intervals, and compared with the fixed power law Z = 230 R^1.25.

## Numbers we use

- **Earlier, unconditional matching (Calheiros and Zawadzki 1987):** section 1, Eq. (1). `∫₀^Zi Pu(Ze) dZe = ∫₀^Ri Pu(R) dR`, where `Pu` are unconditional PDFs (zeros included). The paper notes it implicitly assumes that the gauges represent the rainfall depth of the whole radar field (section 5a), which fails for convective rain because the local depth depends mainly on how often storms pass over a gauge.
- **Conditional matching (Atlas et al. 1990b):** section 1, Eq. (2). `∫_Ri^∞ Pc(R) dR = ∫_Zei^∞ Pc[f(Ze)] dZe`, with `Pc = P(R | R > 0)`; the pairs `(Ri, Zei)` at equal percentiles form the Ze–R function `f`. The earlier version truncated Ze at 21 dBZ and fitted lognormal PDFs, hence power laws (section 1, item 3; section 8).
- **Why rain frequency must be matched separately:** section 5a, Eq. (3). `E(R) = Φ · E(R | R > 0)`, with `Φ` the fraction of the space–time domain with rain. The conditional PDF of R for a given regime is taken as the same everywhere in the radar field ("no hot spots"), while `Φ` varies from gauge to gauge; so `Φ` and the conditional PDF are matched by two separate constraints (section 8, constraints 1 and 2).
- **Step 1, threshold pair (wet-fraction match):** section 5a, Eq. (4); appendix B, step 1. `∫_Rτ^∞ Pu(R) dR = ∫_Zτ^∞ Pu(Z) dZ`, computed on unconditional PDFs of *coincident* radar and gauge data only. `Rτ` is a low but accurately measurable gauge rate; `Zτ` is the reflectivity whose exceedance frequency equals the frequency of `R > Rτ`. In words: `Zτ` is the reflectivity threshold that gives the radar the same wet fraction as the gauges. The authors stress this is what makes all Ze and R values come from "the same population" (section 5a).
- **Rτ = 1.78 mm/h:** section 5a, p. 58. PDFs are stored in 1 dB bins centred on integer dBR (`dBR = 10 log R`) and dBZ; `Rτ` was taken as the 3 dBR bin, whose lower edge is 2.5 dBR = 10^0.25 = 1.78 mm/h. The fraction of time with `R > Rτ` was insensitive to the gauge averaging time (p. 58).
- **Collocation window for step 1:** section 5a, p. 58. Radar side: the bin above the gauge and its eight neighbours in polar coordinates (1° and 1 km apart), i.e. nine radar bins per gauge value, at radar times about every 15 min. Gauge side: 3 min rain rate averaged from the start of the radar scan.
- **Zτ values:** Table 3 (p. 58), dBZ at `Rτ` = 1.78 mm/h, for ranges 20–49 / 50–79 / 80–109 / 110–139 km: monsoon 24 / 27 / 27 / 27; convective maritime 23 / 27 / 27 / 34; squall lines 27 / 29 / 30 / 34; isolated convection 31 / 35 / 38 / 43. The threshold rises with range and with reflectivity gradients, by up to 12 dB within one regime.
- **Step 2, matching above and below the threshold:** section 5a, Eqs. (5a)–(5b). `∫₀^Rτ Pc(R) dR = ∫₀^Zτ Pc(Z) dZ` and `∫_Rτ^∞ Pc(R) dR = ∫_Zτ^∞ Pc(Z) dZ`. Above `Zτ` the radar PDF comes from the *entire* radar field in each range ring, not only from the bins over the gauges (section 7, item 2); the gauge PDF is pooled over all gauges. The relation is unique because Ze and R are assumed to increase monotonically together (p. 58).
- **Matching in practice:** appendix B, Eq. (B7); section 5a, Fig. 3. Counts `nZ` per integer dBZ and `nR` per integer dBR are cumulated into CDFs, and each `Zi` is assigned the `Ri` with `CDF(Zi) = CDF(Ri)`. Fig. 3 normalises both CDFs so that the frequency above `Zτ` and above `Rτ` is one; example (Fig. 3c, squall lines, 110–139 km): CDF = 0.5 maps 35 dBZ to 7 dBR (5 mm/h).
- **Unconditional form with a frequency ratio:** appendix B, Eqs. (B1), (B3)–(B4). If radar values come from the whole field, `Φ ∫_Zτ^∞ Pu(Z) dZ = ∫_Rτ^∞ Pu(R) dR`, where `Φ` is the ratio between the rain fraction over the gauges and over the radar field. The collocated threshold pair `(Rτ, Zτ)` is needed only to fix `Φ`; then `Φ ∫_Zi^∞ Pu(Z) dZ = ∫_Ri^∞ Pu(R) dR` gives the whole relation (Eq. B4).
- **Step 3, the zero-rain reflectivity Z0:** appendix B, steps 1–5, Eqs. (B5)–(B6), (B8). First guess `R0 = 0.2 mm/h` (gauge minimum detectable rate) and `Z0 = 12 dBZ` (radar minimum detectable signal). Match the first moment between `(Z0, Zτ)` and `(R0, Rτ)` and compute `ΔRV = ∫_Z0^Zτ f(Z) Pc(Z) dZ − ∫_R0^Rτ R Pc(R) dR`. If `ΔRV > 0`, the radar adds more rain than the gauges when the threshold is lowered, so `Z0` is raised; if `ΔRV < 0`, `R0` is raised; iterate until `ΔRV ≈ 0`. In Darwin `ΔRV` was always positive, so `R0` stayed at 0.2 mm/h and only `Z0` moved. Reflectivities below `Z0` are mapped to zero rain: they are echoes that, on average, do not correspond to rain at the ground (beam spreading, partial beam filling, sidelobes; Fig. 4). In the figures `Z0` is read where the Ze–R curve meets R = 0.1 mm/h (section 8).
- **Zeroth versus first moment:** section 5b, Eq. (6). Matching counts (zeroth moment) and matching rain volume (first moment, iterated) gave practically the same relation; the iteration converged in fewer than five steps (p. 59).
- **Example relation:** section 6, Fig. 5b. Convective maritime, 50–79 and 80–109 km: 50 dBZ ↔ 125 mm/h and 25 dBZ ↔ 1.1 mm/h, close to `Ze = 250 R^1.20`; reflectivities below 24 dBZ ↔ zero rain. The upper end is fixed by the highest pair (60 dBZ ↔ 150 mm/h).
- **Classification:** section 4a–b, Table 2, appendix A. Four rain regimes defined by synoptic situation: monsoon (westerly, weak convection, weak gradients), convective maritime (north–north-east, moderate), continental squall lines (east–south-east, night, strong, ducting/AP), isolated convection (east, afternoon, over land and islands, large gradients). Radar PDFs are stratified by four range rings of 30 km between 20 and 140 km (section 4b); gauge PDFs are not, because range stratification showed "only small and apparently random variations" (section 4c).
- **Reproducibility of the gauge PDF:** section 4c, Fig. 2 (3 min gauge data, five SOPs). Median volume rain rate and share of rain volume below 10 mm/h: monsoon 13–27 mm/h and 26–44%; convective maritime 23–40 mm/h and 15–30%; squall lines 25–35 mm/h and 20–26%; isolated convection 40 mm/h (60 mm/h in one SOP, due to a single 115 mm storm in 37 min) and 9–12%. The monsoon shows the largest differences between SOPs, which the authors attribute to a system scale larger than the network and the 3-week SOP (p. 57).
- **Sample sizes:** Fig. 3 legends. Gauge 3 min samples: monsoon 30,944; convective maritime 11,715; squall lines 9,677; isolated convection 2,261. Radar samples per range ring: about 45,000–210,000 (isolated convection, convective maritime, squall lines) and about 590,000–711,000 (monsoon).
- **Radar:** section 2, Table 1. NOAA–TOGA C-band Doppler radar (5.33 cm, 2.4 m antenna, 1.65° beamwidth, horizontal polarisation) at 12.25°S, 130.56° (printed "W", Darwin is east) during Special Observing Periods (SOPs) of 3 weeks, 5 min base scans and 5–15 min volume scans. Product used: 1.5 km CAPPI, from the 0.8° low-PRF base scan beyond 48 km and from the volume scan within 48 km (also to avoid ground clutter); bins 1 km × 1°. Sphere calibration before each SOP to within 0.5 dB. Minimum detectable signal 12 dBZ at 100 km; all values below 12 dBZ rejected. Corrections: +0.7 dBZ for the 17.5% of energy intercepted by the ground; gaseous attenuation; AP scans removed by manual inspection. Not corrected: rain attenuation, wet radome; the authors argue that PMM absorbs their average effect (section 2f). The long-pulse base scan was found afterwards to read about 2.5 dBZ higher than the volume scans (p. 53, p. 66).
- **Gauges:** section 3, Fig. 1. 22 tipping-bucket gauges, 0.2 mm tips timed to the second, up to 126 km from the radar, ±1% up to 375 mm/h; tips smoothed, turned into 1 min rates, then 3 min running means. The Ze–R functions refer to this 3 min averaging time (section 3b; section 8).
- **Period:** appendix A, Tables A1–A4. Rain periods between day 355 of 1987 and day 48 of 1988 (about 21 December 1987 to 17 February 1988), AP periods excluded; Fig. 2 also uses SOPs of the 1988–89 season (SOP numbers 881–883). Disdrometer data of 1989–90 are used only for Fig. 7.
- **Test against a fixed Z–R:** section 7a, Eqs. (7)–(9), Table 4. Radar rain in the bin above each gauge, accumulated over the 5 min window around each radar base scan, with the PMM functions and with `Z = 230 R^1.25`; `Bp`, `Bf` are mean radar/gauge ratios; `Sp`, `Sf` are rain-weighted geometric standard deviations of the per-gauge ratios. The test uses the same data that built the relations; the authors argue it is still meaningful because PMM matches PDFs over the whole field and does not force the bias at the gauges to one (section 7, items 1–2).
- **Bias results:** section 7a(1), Table 4, Fig. 8. All regimes, SOPs 2 and 3, ranges centred at 31 / 66 / 92 / 119 km: `Bp` = 0.98 / 1.13 / 1.02 / 0.88 against `Bf` = 0.63 / 1.19 / 1.15 / 2.05. For gauges with at least 500 min of rain (`TG > 500`), `Bp` lies between 0.88 and 1.15, standard deviation 0.09. With the fixed law, isolated convection reaches `Bf` = 2.15 / 3.58 / 4.03 at 66 / 92 / 119 km (PMM: 1.10 / 1.69 / 0.86), attributed to partial beam filling.
- **Scatter results:** section 7a(2), Table 4, Fig. 10. All regimes: `Sp` = 1.26 / 1.20 / 1.45 / 1.71 against `Sf` = 1.26 / 1.29 / 1.59 / 1.82 (Table 4); the text gives 20–26% within 80 km and 45% and 71% at 92 and 119 km, but a few lines later quotes `Sp` as "1.26, 1.20, 1.45, 1.45", which disagrees with Table 4 at 119 km. Reduction of the error relative to the mean bias by up to 30% at medium and long ranges (p. 67); "about 30%" beyond 50 km (section 8). For isolated convection `Sp` is larger than `Sf` at some ranges (Table 4: 2.12 vs 1.82 at 31 km; 4.01 vs 2.81 at 119 km), explained by point-comparison geometry and timing errors in strong gradients (p. 67).
- **Sample needed:** section 8, p. 69. The standard deviation of the biases of individual classes is below 10% when a class has at least 500 min of rain above about 2 mm/h; the bias is "roughly inversely proportional" to sample size.
- **Space–time domain:** section 7b. Not investigated; a rough estimate from Texas (Rosenfeld et al. 1990) is a domain of 10^4 km² for a 7% standard error, equivalent to 3–8 storm clusters or one storm sweeping about 1,700 km² in about 2 h; tropical rain (Florida, Lopez et al. 1989) may need less. The validity of PMM is stated for climatic scales, not single points or times (section 8).

## Relevance to DECADE

H3 (#20), harmonisation of ODYSSEY and CIRRUS through a bridge dataset.

- **Frequency matched first, then quantiles:** PMM is the radar precedent for this order. Eq. (4) fixes, from coincident data, the reflectivity threshold `Zτ` at which the radar has the same wet fraction as the reference; Eqs. (5)/(B7) then match the conditional distributions rank by rank. For H3 this means: per product and per season, find the dBZ threshold whose exceedance frequency on the bridge grid equals the wet frequency of the bridge (at its own wet threshold), map everything below to zero, and quantile-map only the wet part. Each product gets its own threshold, which is how the ODYSSEY–CIRRUS gap in echo frequency (15.6% vs 22.6% of Belgian pixels ≥ 10 dBZ on the 4 common days) would be absorbed, while the small shift among common echoes (0.5–1.5 dB) is left to the quantile step. In Darwin, too, much of the radar–gauge difference ends up in the threshold: `Zτ` varies by up to 12 dB with range within one regime (Table 3), and `Z0` grows with range and reflectivity gradients (section 6a).
- **Mapping without time pairing:** only the threshold pair needs collocated, simultaneous data; the conditional distributions are pooled over the domain and matched by rank (sections 5a, 7; appendix B). This supports an H3 design in which each product is mapped to the bridge separately, using their full overlap for the wet fraction and pooled seasonal distributions for the quantiles, without needing ODYSSEY and CIRRUS to overlap in time. When the frequency is estimated on a subset of the domain, Eq. (B1) shows the correction needed (the ratio `Φ`).
- **Need for a stable reference:** PMM works only if the conditional distribution of the reference is reproducible for the chosen class and domain (section 4c, section 5a: no "hot spots"). Darwin gauges met this for squall lines and isolated convection, not for the monsoon, whose scale exceeded network and SOP (p. 57). For H3 the bridge plays the gauge role: it must have the same distribution in the ODYSSEY years and in the CIRRUS years, which a reanalysis or a gauge-merged product with constant inputs is more likely to provide than either radar product. Its seasonal stratification is the analogue of the rain-regime stratification here.
- **Sample size as a check:** the "500 min of rain above ~2 mm/h for < 10% bias scatter" rule (section 8) and the ~10^4 km² domain estimate (section 7b) are the only quantitative hints on how much wet data a matched class needs; with CIRRUS from 7/2024 only, seasonal classes should be checked against something of this order, adapted to the bridge resolution.
- **Zero-rain echoes:** the `Z0` iteration (appendix B) is a published rule for deciding which weak echoes correspond, on average, to no rain at the ground. It matches the first moment (rain volume) rather than the frequency; the paper found both give the same result in Darwin (section 5b).

## Limits

- One C-band radar with a 1.65° beam and 22 gauges, not a multi-radar composite; the relations are tied to that radar's beamwidth, wavelength, sensitivity and attenuation, and the authors say they should not be transferred to other radars without care (sections 6, 8). In a composite, beam geometry varies from pixel to pixel and from radar to radar, and is mixed by the compositing rule.
- Tropical rain near Darwin (monsoon and continental convection, wet season 1987–88; Florida appears only as a citation in section 7b); Belgian stratiform and frontal rain, with bright band and winter snow, is not tested.
- 1980s–1990s data: 1.5 km CAPPI from a single radar, manual AP editing, 12 dBZ cut-off, 3 min gauges, radar every ~15 min. Not 2 km / 15 min ODYSSEY or 1 km / 5 min CIRRUS composites, nor a reanalysis or gauge-merged bridge.
- The matching is reflectivity to gauge rain rate, with rain-regime classes chosen by hand from the synoptic situation; H3 maps reflectivity to a gridded reference and stratifies by season.
- The evaluation reuses the data that built the relations (section 7); there is no independent period, and the authors do not claim validity outside the analysed periods (section 8).
- The minimum space–time domain is not investigated (section 7b); the sample-size rule (section 8) is stated for gauge minutes in Darwin and does not convert directly to bridge grid cells and hours.
- Inconsistency in section 7a(2): `Sp` at 119 km is 1.71 in Table 4 and in one sentence, but 1.45 in another.
