# A radar-based assessment of the detectability of giant hail

- **Authors, year, venue:** Blair, S. F., Deroche, D. R., Boustead, J. M., Leighton, J. W., Barjenbruch, B. L., Gargan, W. P., 2011. Electronic Journal of Severe Storms Meteorology 6(7), 1–30.
- **Link:** [ejssm.org PDF](https://ejssm.org/archives/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/vol6-7.pdf)
- **Access:** free (open-access e-journal); no licence statement in the extracted text.
- **Read:** full text, focusing on section 2 (data and method), the results on maximum reflectivity (Figs. 6–8, 20) and the reviewer comments appended to the paper.

## What it is

The study takes 568 US reports of giant hail (≥ 102 mm) from 1995–2009 and examines the S-band WSR-88D volume of each parent storm around the report time. It records, among other things, the maximum reflectivity in the storm column and the heights of the 50–75 dBZ echoes. The authors conclude that maximum reflectivity does not separate giant hail from smaller hail.

## Numbers we use

- **Median maximum reflectivity of 69 dBZ:** across all 568 giant-hail storms, taken from the full radar volume (text on Fig. 6). The full distribution is only shown in the figure.
- **A 75 dBZ echo was present in 26 of 568 storms:** Fig. 7 caption; 70 dBZ was present in 220 storms.
- **65 % of the storms with a maximum ≥ 75 dBZ fell in 2008–2009:** these two years had super-resolution data and make up 17 % of the database (results text near Fig. 6). Finer bins raise the observed maxima.
- **80–82 dBZ:** a reviewer comment (Reviewer C, L. R. Lemon), not the authors' result. The reviewer has seen values this high with super-resolution data, whereas legacy data "very rarely" reached 78–79 dBZ. This is secondary and anecdotal.
- **Reflectivity ≥ 65 dBZ was common** for both giant hail and 45–51 mm hail (Fig. 20 text).

## Relevance to DECADE

H2 (#19), impossible values: this source supplies the physical ceiling. Even in the most extreme hailstorms, measured at S-band, the storm maximum is typically about 70 dBZ, values ≥ 75 dBZ are rare, and about 80 dBZ is the anecdotal extreme. Values ≥ 100 dBZ, such as the 128 dBZ on 3 July 2024, are therefore far beyond anything meteorological. A discard threshold somewhere in 75–80 dBZ would keep essentially every genuine echo.

## Limits

- The radars are S-band WSR-88D; Belgium and OPERA are mostly C-band. Resonance and attenuation effects for wet hail differ at C-band, and this paper does not cover them.
- The values come from full-volume, storm-scale maxima at native resolution. A 2 km composite pixel is usually lower, but OPERA's column maximum takes the highest bin from any elevation.
- The paper sets no QC threshold. It only describes observed extremes in a sample of US supercells.
